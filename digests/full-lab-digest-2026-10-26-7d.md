### Resazurin as a metabolic-rate proxy across temperatures in Pacific oysters

**Source:** genefish WordPress (Samuel Slutz)
**Finding:** Resazurin-based metabolic assays on M. gigas across 30°C, 21°C (shaken/unshaken), and 11°C found metabolic activity highest at 30°C and lowest at 11°C, with anomalous negative-area readings in the 21°C unshaken plate needing follow-up.

### Supports [PubMed]: From blue to pink: resazurin as a high-throughput proxy for metabolic rate in oysters
Huffmyer AS et al., 2026 · PMID: 42495017 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42495017/ (originally posted as bioRxiv preprint: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.11.06.686367)

This peer-reviewed study, from the same lab group, validates a whole-organism resazurin fluorescence assay as a reliable proxy for metabolic rate in *Crassostrea gigas* and *C. virginica*, showing strong correlation with oxygen consumption. Thermal performance trials revealed clear temperature-dependent metabolic optima and tipping points, where stimulation at moderate temperatures shifted to metabolic depression at higher temperatures — consistent with Samuel Slutz's observation of highest metabolic activity at 30°C and lowest at 11°C. The authors also found that greater metabolic depression under acute thermal stress predicted higher mortality risk, suggesting resazurin readings could help flag which individuals are more vulnerable in the lab's ongoing heat-stress trials.

**Literature summary:** One peer-reviewed paper (also previously circulated as a bioRxiv preprint) directly validates resazurin as a temperature-sensitive metabolic proxy in oysters, supporting the pattern seen in this week's M. gigas metabolic trials and reinforcing resazurin's use as a tool for anticipating thermal-stress vulnerability across the lab's ongoing heat-stress work.

### Immune/stress priming and thermal tolerance in Pacific oysters

**Source:** genefish WordPress (giselff250455090's H2O2-priming heat-stress experiment) and Megan Ewing's notebook (candidate HSP/Viperin qPCR gene list)
**Finding:** Oysters pre-primed with low or high H2O2 doses and then held at 36°C reached complete mortality by 96 hours, with deaths spread across all priming groups rather than concentrated in one; separately, Megan is compiling a candidate gene list for bivalve stress/immune-priming qPCR work that includes HSP70, HSP90, and Viperin.

### Adds context [Preprint — not peer-reviewed]: Parental immune priming reshapes offspring growth, metabolism, and thermal tolerance in the Pacific Oyster
Baird M et al., 2025 · DOI: 10.64898/2025.12.10.693539 · https://doi.org/10.64898/2025.12.10.693539

This not-yet-peer-reviewed preprint tested whether parental immune challenge (Poly(I:C)) in Pacific oysters alters offspring thermal tolerance, finding temperature-dependent and even reversing effects: primed offspring had 35% lower mortality than controls at 40°C but 17% higher mortality at 42°C, with differential expression of heat-shock genes (HSP70, HSP90) and the immune gene viperin under acute stress. This supports Megan's inclusion of HSP70/HSP90/viperin as qPCR targets for priming studies, and offers a possible explanation for the WordPress H2O2-priming result: because priming benefits in this preprint were narrowly temperature- and stressor-specific, a lack of protective effect from H2O2 priming at 36°C under a different priming paradigm is plausible rather than necessarily a failed experiment.

**Literature summary:** One preprint — not yet peer-reviewed — directly ties heat-shock (HSP70/HSP90) and immune (viperin) gene expression to immune-priming outcomes in Pacific oyster thermal-stress trials, lending literature support to Megan's candidate gene choices and offering a possible, temperature-dependent explanation for this week's uniform H2O2-priming mortality pattern. This should be weighed cautiously given its preprint status.
