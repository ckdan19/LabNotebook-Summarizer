## 10.09-10: Sample point 2 for repeat stress at 35C

**Source:** HazelAbrahamsonA, 2026-10-10 · https://genefish.wordpress.com/2026/10/10/10-09-10-sample-point-2-for-repeat-stress-at-35c/
**Finding:** In a repeat 35°C heat/no-heat stress experiment with four treatment groups based on prior exposure history (heat/heat, no heat/heat, heat/no heat, no heat/no heat), mortality after the second 24-hour exposure was similar across all groups (27-36%), suggesting no strong protective or sensitizing effect of a prior heat exposure (with a week of recovery) on survival of a subsequent heat challenge.

### Adds context [PubMed]: Microbial endolithic symbiosis in oysters enhances thermal resistance through shell corrosion
Zardi et al., 2026 · PMID: 42430847 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42430847/

This study tested whether symbiotic endolithic organisms that corrode Pacific oyster (Magallana gigas) shells buffer the animals against heat stress. Laboratory and field experiments found that corroded oysters survived significantly better under thermal stress, with a buffering effect as large as 9.5°C, linked to lighter, more reflective shell coloration from the corrosion. For the lab's repeat-stress result, this suggests that survival outcomes under heat challenge in this species can hinge heavily on an individual-level factor (shell microbiome/corrosion state) unrelated to prior heat exposure history, which could help explain why mortality looked similar across all four exposure-history treatment groups — a symbiont-driven thermal buffer could be swamping any priming effect from the first heat exposure.

### Adds context [bioRxiv preprint — not peer-reviewed]: Parental immune priming reshapes offspring growth, metabolism, and thermal tolerance in the Pacific Oyster
Baird et al., 2025 · DOI: 10.64898/2025.12.10.693539 · https://doi.org/10.64898/2025.12.10.693539

This preprint tested whether parental immune challenge (Poly(I:C)) in Pacific oysters shifts offspring survival, growth, and metabolism under subsequent heat stress. It found that priming effects on mortality were temperature-dependent and could even reverse sign: offspring of primed parents had 35% lower mortality than controls at 40°C, but 17% higher mortality at 42°C, alongside shifts in metabolic activity and heat-shock/immune gene expression. This is relevant context for the lab's repeat-stress design: it shows that prior-stress effects on heat-stress mortality in this species are not simply "protective" or "sensitizing" but can flip across a narrow temperature window, which raises the possibility that a single fixed temperature (35°C) could mask a priming effect that would appear at a different thermal challenge level — though this preprint primes with immune challenge across generations rather than heat within one generation, so it is not a direct test of the same manipulation.

**Literature summary:** No peer-reviewed or preprint literature from the last 12 months directly tests repeated heat exposure (heat-priming a single generation of oysters and then re-exposing them) and its effect on subsequent mortality in Pacific oysters. One peer-reviewed paper and one preprint each offer indirect context: the peer-reviewed study shows that non-stress-history factors (symbiont-driven shell corrosion) can drive large differences in heat-stress survival, and the preprint (not yet peer-reviewed) shows that prior-stress priming effects on heat-stress mortality in this species can be temperature-dependent and even reverse direction across a few degrees. Together these suggest plausible reasons why the repeat-stress treatment groups showed similar mortality, but neither paper measures the same same-generation heat-preconditioning design as the lab post.
