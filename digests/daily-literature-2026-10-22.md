**Finding:** After approximately 72 hours at 36°C, a mortality assay found 13 of 17 oysters dead and only 4 still alive, with deaths occurring across all treatment groups (high, control, and low) rather than concentrated in one group.

### Adds context [PubMed]: Microbial endolithic symbiosis in oysters enhances thermal resistance through shell corrosion
Zardi et al., 2026 · PMID: 42430847 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42430847/

This lab and field study examined whether endolithic microbial symbionts that corrode Pacific oyster (Magallana gigas) shells provide a thermal buffering effect, finding that corroded oysters survived heat stress significantly better than uncorroded ones, with a buffering effect as large as 9.5°C in the field tied to lighter, more reflective shell coloration from the corrosion. For the lab's incubator-based mortality assay, this suggests that uncontrolled variation in shell condition or symbiont load among individual oysters could itself contribute to the uneven, cross-group mortality pattern observed, independent of the treatment groups being tested.

### Supports [PubMed]: Integrated metabolomics and flow cytometry assessment reveals immune-metabolic modulation of Crassostrea (=Magallana) gigas hemolymph under hypoxic and elevated temperature conditions
Kim et al., 2026 · PMID: 42134734 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42134734/

Using Pacific oysters exposed to hypoxia alone or combined with elevated temperature (28°C) for 13 days, this study found hemocyte mortality was significantly higher under the combined hypoxia-plus-heat condition than under hypoxia alone, alongside metabolic shifts suggesting energy conservation came at the cost of immune function. This supports the general pattern that elevated temperature exacerbates mortality risk in Pacific oysters, consistent with the lab's observation of substantial mortality after sustained 36°C incubation, though this study isolates temperature's interaction with hypoxia rather than heat alone.

### Supports [PubMed]: Short-term heat stress adaptation in intertidal oysters (Crassostrea sikamea): Integrative biochemical, transcriptomic and metabolomic insights
Liu et al., 2026 · PMID: 41401620 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41401620/

This study on Kumamoto oysters ran short-term survival assays across a temperature gradient and found that while mortality stayed low below 45°C, a 1-hour exposure at 45°C already dropped survival to 14%, with any higher temperature or longer exposure proving uniformly lethal; transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling identified thousands of heat-responsive genes and hundreds of altered metabolites during this rapid decline. This closely parallels the lab's own finding of steep, sudden mortality (13 of 17 dead) after prolonged incubation at a comparably extreme temperature (36°C for ~72 hours), supporting the idea that sustained thermal stress near an oyster's upper tolerance limit produces abrupt, large-scale mortality rather than a gradual decline.

### Adds context [EcoEvoRxiv preprint — not peer-reviewed]: Intertidal Exposure Modulates Time-Integrated Heat Tolerance of the Eastern Oyster Crassostrea virginica
Villeneuve et al., 2026 · DOI: 10.32942/x2cw9d · https://doi.org/10.32942/x2cw9d

This preprint developed thermal death time curves for the eastern oyster under both submerged and air-exposed conditions, then used them to build a dynamic survival model predicting cumulative mortality based on exposure duration and temperature regime, validated against field survival data. This framework is directly relevant to interpreting the lab's ~72-hour continuous 36°C exposure, since it suggests that exposure duration, not just peak temperature, is a major driver of how much mortality accumulates by a given timepoint.
