- **AI-use disclosure**: Level 0 (None)
- **Key finding**: This post presents initial growth and survival analysis for the 10K Seed Hardening oyster outplant experiment at Baywater, comparing control, heat (35°C), freshwater, combined freshwater/heat, and Poly(I:C) immune-exposure treatments across four time points spanning late 2024 to late 2026. ### Water-chemistry trial tank tolerance to elevated ammonia/nitrite/nitrate (genefish WordPress)

**Source:** Genefish WordPress (Jesse Lowe's tank room / water-chemistry trial logs; Cas Daniel's automated Daily Literature Connections posts)
**Finding:** A dedicated water-chemistry trial tank maintained at markedly elevated ammonia (≥0.25 ppm), nitrite (0.5–1.5 ppm), and nitrate (120–200 ppm) with low pH (7.4) showed oysters largely thriving, with only 2 mortalities recorded since the trial began.

### [Adds context] [Research Square preprint — not peer-reviewed]: The Effects of High Carbonate Alkalinity under Low Salinity on the Survival, Physiological Metabolism, and Redox Homeostasis of Crassostrea hongkongensis
Nan X et al., 2026 · DOI: 10.21203/rs.3.rs-10038928/v1 · https://doi.org/10.21203/rs.3.rs-10038928/v1

Exposed Hong Kong oysters (Crassostrea hongkongensis) to low salinity combined with elevated carbonate alkalinity (10 or 20 mmol/L) and measured survival, feeding, oxygen consumption, ammonia excretion, and redox/energy biomarkers. The higher-alkalinity treatment suppressed oxygen consumption and ammonia excretion and significantly reduced survival, while the lower-alkalinity treatment retained partial compensatory capacity. This offers relevant context for the lab's trial tank: it shows that in a related oyster species, impaired ammonia excretion under hydrochemical stress can precede measurable survival loss — suggesting the trial tank's currently low mortality count may not fully capture slower-building physiological strain — though the specific stressor combination (carbonate alkalinity vs. accumulated ammonia/nitrite/nitrate) differs from the lab's setup.

**Literature summary:** One preprint (not yet peer-reviewed) was found addressing bivalve physiological responses to hydrochemical stress involving ammonia dynamics; no peer-reviewed PubMed papers specifically matched this finding. The preprint's results on Crassostrea hongkongensis suggest ammonia-excretion suppression can be an early sign of hydrochemical stress that precedes outright survival loss, offering a cautious note for interpreting the lab's trial tank's currently stable survival despite degraded water chemistry — though species and exact stressor composition differ.
