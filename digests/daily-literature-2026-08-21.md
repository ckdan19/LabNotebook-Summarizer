## Tank room, heat exposures, and resazurin assays

**Source:** Jesse Lowe, 2026-08-20 · https://genefish.wordpress.com/2026/08/20/tank-room-heat-exposures-and-resazurin-assays/
**Finding:** The first round of oyster heat exposures was ended after all of the oysters had died — complete mortality under sustained thermal stress — clearing the water table to begin the next exposure round.

### Supports [PubMed]: Short-term heat stress adaptation in intertidal oysters (Crassostrea sikamea): Integrative biochemical, transcriptomic and metabolomic insights
Liu et al., 2026 · PMID: 41401620 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41401620/

This study exposed intertidal Kumamoto oysters to a gradient of high temperatures while tracking survival alongside transcriptomic and metabolomic profiling. Survival collapsed at the upper extreme — only 14% of oysters survived one hour at 45 °C and none survived above that, while mortality stayed minimal at lower temperatures — pinpointing a sharp lethal threshold. That abrupt shift to total mortality mirrors the lab's observation that a sustained heat exposure eventually killed every oyster, and points to the kind of narrow lethal-temperature window that likely drove the die-off.

### Adds context [PubMed]: From blue to pink: resazurin as a high-throughput proxy for metabolic rate in oysters
Huffmyer et al., 2026 · PMID: 42495017 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42495017/ (originally posted as bioRxiv preprint: https://doi.org/10.1101/2025.11.06.686367)

Working in the same *Crassostrea gigas*/*C. virginica* system — and with the resazurin assay the lab itself was running the same day — this study validated whole-organism resazurin fluorescence against oxygen consumption and mapped metabolic responses across temperatures. It identified clear thermal optima and tipping points where metabolism flips from stimulation to depression, and found that oysters showing greater metabolic depression under acute thermal stress were more likely to survive. That frames metabolism as a measurable predictor of who lives or dies under heat, offering a physiological readout that could help anticipate a complete-mortality outcome like the one observed.

### Adds context [PubMed]: Effects of temperature and Nocardia crassostreae on the immune response of the Pacific oyster, Magallana gigas
Mason et al., 2026 · PMID: 42476329 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42476329/

This experiment held Pacific oysters at elevated temperature, with and without the bacterium *Nocardia crassostreae*, for 42 days and then measured energetic and immune parameters. Elevated temperature on its own depleted glycogen and ATP, reduced haemocyte viability, and impaired gill Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase activity — evidence that sustained warming erodes energy balance and cellular integrity even before a pathogen is involved. It contextualizes how prolonged thermal stress alone can progressively weaken oysters toward death, consistent with a heat-exposure round ending in total mortality.

### Adds context [PubMed]: Microbial endolithic symbiosis in oysters enhances thermal resistance through shell corrosion
Zardi et al., 2026 · PMID: 42430847 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42430847/

This work tested whether shell-boring endolithic symbionts buffer Pacific oysters against heat, using both laboratory and field heat-stress experiments. Corroded oysters survived significantly better, with a thermal buffer as large as 9.5 °C in the field, linking shell optical changes to a reduced heat load. The large survival swing shows how close oysters can sit to their lethal thermal limit and how a single modifying factor can decide survival versus mortality — a useful lens on why an unbuffered lab exposure produced complete death.

### Adds context [PubMed]: Identifying the regulatory network of the key lipid metabolism transcription factor PPAR in oysters
Du et al., 2026 · PMID: 42331158 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42331158/

This study dissected the regulatory network of PPARα, a lipid-metabolism transcription factor tied to thermal adaptation, comparing the relatively cold-tolerant *C. gigas* with *C. angulata*. It found higher PPARα expression and promoter activity in the more heat-hardy species and identified SNPs and regulatory genes underlying that difference, all framed against a backdrop of summer mass-mortality events. It suggests that genotype-driven differences in thermal-tolerance machinery help set where an oyster's lethal threshold falls.

### Adds context [PubMed]: Integrated metabolomics and flow cytometry reveals immune-metabolic modulation of Crassostrea (=Magallana) gigas hemolymph under hypoxic and elevated temperature conditions
Kim et al., 2026 · PMID: 42134734 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42134734/

This study exposed Pacific oysters to hypoxia alone and to hypoxia combined with elevated temperature (28 °C) for 13 days, profiling hemolymph metabolites and hemocyte phenotypes. Combined heat-and-hypoxia stress raised hemocyte mortality and suppressed immune function through a shift toward anaerobic, energy-conserving metabolism, which the authors link to summer mortality in Pacific oysters. It highlights that thermal stress rarely acts alone and that its lethal effects can be amplified by co-occurring low oxygen — a factor worth checking in any tank die-off.

### Suggests next step [EcoEvoRxiv preprint — not peer-reviewed]: Intertidal Exposure Modulates Time-Integrated Heat Tolerance of the Eastern Oyster Crassostrea virginica
Villeneuve et al., 2026 · DOI: 10.32942/x2cw9d · https://doi.org/10.32942/x2cw9d

This preprint built thermal death time (TDT) curves for eastern oysters under both submerged and aerial exposure, using heart rate to mark time-to-death, then folded those curves into a tidal survival model. It shows that lethal outcomes depend jointly on temperature and duration (and on exposure type — the tolerance ranking flips above 37 °C), and that the resulting framework predicts field survival well. For the lab, this points to a concrete next step: characterizing the exposure as a temperature-by-time dose could turn "all oysters died" into a quantified lethal threshold that is comparable across experiments.

### Adds context [bioRxiv preprint — not peer-reviewed]: Parental immune priming reshapes offspring growth, metabolism, and thermal tolerance in the Pacific Oyster
Baird et al., 2025 · DOI: 10.64898/2025.12.10.693539 · https://doi.org/10.64898/2025.12.10.693539

This preprint (from the same lab group) challenged Pacific oyster broodstock with Poly(I:C), reared their offspring to the seed stage, and tested survival and metabolism under thermal stress. Offspring of primed parents had 35% lower mortality than controls at 40 °C but 17% higher mortality at 42 °C, showing both that Pacific oysters hit steep mortality gradients in the 40–42 °C range and that parental history shifts where that threshold lands. It brackets the temperatures at which Pacific-oyster die-offs occur and flags parental/priming background as a variable that could influence how completely a cohort succumbs.

**Literature summary:** Across six peer-reviewed papers and two preprints, the recent literature consistently shows that oysters carry a sharp upper thermal limit beyond which survival collapses — most directly in a peer-reviewed Kumamoto-oyster study reporting 0% survival above 45 °C, which supports the lab's complete die-off under sustained heat. The other peer-reviewed work (all 2026) maps the physiological and molecular routes to that endpoint: metabolic depression as a survival predictor, energy and immune erosion under prolonged warming, and symbiont- and genotype-linked modifiers of the lethal threshold. Two preprints (EcoEvoRxiv and bioRxiv, not peer-reviewed) add a quantitative thermal-death-time framework for converting a die-off into a temperature-by-time threshold and place Pacific-oyster mortality gradients in the 40–42 °C window. Together they suggest the observed total mortality reflects a genuine lethal-temperature threshold — and that logging the exposure temperature and duration would let the lab pinpoint it.
