### Citrate synthase as an aerobic-capacity readout in heat-stressed bivalve tissue

**Source:** Sam's Notebook (Sam White)
**Finding:** Citrate synthase rates in Family 5 ctenidia clustered tightly at both ambient (6.8–8.6 mA412/min) and 36 °C (2.13–2.30 mA412/min), but the two plates were read for different durations (40 min vs 20 min), so the ambient-vs-36 °C comparison does not sample the same portion of the kinetic curve.

### Adds context [PubMed]: Energy metabolism under thermal stress in *Mytilus galloprovincialis* and *Ruditapes decussatus*: Insights from gene expression and enzyme activity profiles
Papadopoulos et al., 2025 · PMID: 40627889 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40627889/

This study tracked citrate synthase activity alongside HOAD and LDH, and pyruvate kinase and PEPCK transcription, in a mussel and a clam across a 25-day ramp to increasing temperatures. Both species held a restrained response to mild warming and shifted progressively toward anaerobic pathways at intermediate and high temperatures, with the mussel suppressing aerobic metabolism outright at peak temperature while the clam raised both aerobic and anaerobic capacity. The key implication for the lab's data is that citrate synthase moves as part of a coordinated aerobic-to-anaerobic shift whose direction and timing are temperature- and species-dependent — so a single-timepoint CS value at one heat dose is difficult to interpret without a paired anaerobic marker or a time course.

**Literature summary:** One peer-reviewed study directly measures citrate synthase activity under thermal stress in bivalves and shows the enzyme responds as part of a time-dependent aerobic/anaerobic rebalancing rather than as a static index of thermal state. This supports the caution already flagged in the notebook about standardizing read duration and assay conditions before comparing across temperatures, and suggests pairing CS with an anaerobic enzyme such as LDH. No relevant preprints on bivalve citrate synthase were retrieved — the preprint search surfaced only vertebrate and microbial TCA-cycle work, which failed the relevance filter.

### Complete mortality within days at 36 °C in the Manchester Hardening survival assay

**Source:** Genefish WordPress
**Finding:** Round 1 of the 36 °C survival assay ran hotter and faster than intended — mortality began within a day, only 14 of 80 oysters remained by the 8/19 evening check, and all were dead by the 8/20 morning check, ending the trial early. Family-level differences in survival were nonetheless already apparent.

### Suggests next step [EcoEvoRxiv preprint — not peer-reviewed]: Intertidal Exposure Modulates Time-Integrated Heat Tolerance of the Eastern Oyster *Crassostrea virginica*
Villeneuve et al., 2026 · DOI: 10.32942/x2cw9d · https://doi.org/10.32942/x2cw9d

The authors built thermal death time curves for the eastern oyster under both submerged and aerial exposure, using heart rate monitoring to establish time to death, and folded those curves into a dynamic survival model that tracked cumulative thermal dose across tidal cycles. Their field-calibrated predictions matched observed survival, and they found the ranking of immersed versus emersed tolerance reverses above 37 °C. The framework is directly applicable to the lab's problem: treating heat tolerance as an integral of temperature over time, rather than a fixed lethal temperature, is what would let a run that reaches complete mortality in three days still yield comparable family-level tolerance estimates.

**Literature summary:** One preprint — not yet peer-reviewed — provides a time-integrated framework for exactly the situation the lab encountered, where a heat dose overshoots and compresses the whole mortality curve into a few days. It suggests that recording time-to-death per individual and fitting thermal death time curves would preserve the family-level signal that a fixed endpoint loses. No peer-reviewed paper on oyster heat-tolerance heritability was retrieved in the last 12 months.

### Heat plus polyIC primed cockle still responsive at 22 hours

**Source:** Genefish WordPress
**Finding:** In the first Agate Pass cockle retrieval, mortality checks at 22 hours found only two survivors, one of which came from the heat plus polyIC primed group and was still moving and responding to stimuli.

### Adds context [PubMed]: The RNA sensor MDA5 contributes to the antiviral immune response in *Crassostrea gigas* by modulating the MAVS-mediated signaling pathway
Xu et al., 2026 · PMID: 41605267 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41605267/

This work identified an MDA5 homologue in the Pacific oyster, showed its transcripts are most abundant in haemocytes and are significantly upregulated by poly(I:C) and 5′-ppp dsRNA, and confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation that it interacts with the oyster MAVS adaptor. Knocking down either MDA5 or MAVS reduced downstream expression of the IRF and IFN-like genes. It establishes that poly(I:C) is not a generic irritant in bivalves but is sensed through a defined receptor cascade, which is what makes a priming treatment mechanistically plausible rather than incidental.

### Adds context [PubMed]: An OASL homologue involved in IFN-like antiviral signal by binding MDA5 in the Pacific oyster *Crassostrea gigas*
Zeng et al., 2026 · PMID: 41580101 · https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41580101/

A companion study identified an OASL homologue in *C. gigas* whose expression in haemocytes rises after poly(I:C) and after recombinant IFN-like protein stimulation, and falls when the IFN-like ligand or its receptor is knocked down. The recombinant protein bound dsRNA and interacted specifically with MDA5 rather than RIG-I. Together with the MDA5 result, this fills in an interferon-like axis downstream of poly(I:C) in oysters, giving a concrete set of transcripts that could be measured to confirm whether a priming treatment actually engaged the pathway in the cockles.

### Adds context [bioRxiv preprint — not peer-reviewed]: Parental immune priming reshapes offspring growth, metabolism, and thermal tolerance in the Pacific Oyster
Baird et al., 2025 · DOI: 10.64898/2025.12.10.693539 · https://doi.org/10.64898/2025.12.10.693539

Broodstock Pacific oysters were given a poly(I:C) immune challenge and their offspring were reared to seed stage and then tested for survival, growth, and metabolic response under heat. Offspring of primed parents were about 6.5% larger by 236 days and had 35% lower mortality at 40 °C, but the advantage reversed at 42 °C where they showed 17% higher mortality than controls. This is the closest published test of the heat-plus-poly(I:C) combination the lab is running, and its central caveat — that the benefit is bounded and can invert at a higher heat dose — bears directly on interpreting a single responsive survivor.

**Literature summary:** Two peer-reviewed papers establish that poly(I:C) in oysters is sensed through a specific MDA5/OASL interferon-like axis, so a priming effect has a defined mechanism behind it rather than being a nonspecific stress response. One preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, tests poly(I:C) priming crossed with thermal stress directly and finds a real but dose-bounded survival benefit that reverses at higher temperature. Taken together the literature makes the lab's observation plausible but not yet evidential: a single responsive animal out of two survivors cannot distinguish a priming effect from the heat dose landing near a threshold where the effect flips sign.
